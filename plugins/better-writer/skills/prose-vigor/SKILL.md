# Prose Vigor

Slack prose leaks energy: passive constructions, vague verbs, piled modifiers, sentences that all open the same way and run the same length. This skill hunts the slack and restores the vigor. Run it on a draft — yours or someone else's. Writing from scratch? Outline your main points, draft loose and fast, then bring the result here.

## 1. Diagnose

Read the whole text through, listening for rhythm, and mark every slack spot — the tells:

- passive voice, or a weak verb doing a strong verb's job
- adjectives, adverbs, and filter words (_I saw, I heard, I felt_) the sentence would survive without
- similes and stock phrases that restate the obvious
- labels for things the reader could instead be shown
- monotone rhythm: consecutive sentences that open the same way or hover at the same length

Repetition hides while you draft and surfaces only on a read-through — this pass is that read-through.

Done when every slack spot in the text is marked.

## 2. Revise

Work each marked spot against the rules below. Restraint governs the pass: a spot already vigorous stays as written, and a page of nothing but short punchy sentences reads as monotone as a page with none. Fix the stretches where sameness dulls the rhythm, not every line.

- **Active voice.** Put the actor first: "The report was written by Jane" → "Jane wrote the report." Keep passive only when the actor genuinely doesn't matter — "The painting was stolen."
- **Strong verbs.** Trade a vague verb (_say, get, move, was walking_) for one precise verb chosen for its tone: _explain, reveal, crept, raced, sauntered_. One strong verb beats a weak verb plus a gerund or a modifier.
- **Cut modifiers.** Delete the adjectives and adverbs the sentence still works without — _very, really, extremely_ go first.
- **Cut filter words.** Drop _I saw / heard / felt / thought_ and render the thing itself: "I heard a loud noise" → "A deafening crash echoed through the room."
- **Rewrite the tired.** Delete a simile that only restates the obvious ("the sun like a giant glowing orb"); keep one only when it ties the abstract to the concrete in a fresh way. Replace any phrase you've read in every other piece on the topic with plainer, more specific words.
- **Be concrete.** Trade labels and generalities for action and detail: "she was frustrated" → "she slammed the laptop shut"; "many users" → "4,000 users."
- **One idea per sentence.** Run out of breath reading it aloud? Split it.
- **Vary length.** Mix short and long on purpose — the short sentence lands the punch; a long one earns its length only when every clause carries weight.
- **Vary openers.** Break the subject-verb, subject-verb march: open sometimes with a dependent clause ("As Mandy watched, the door swung wide"), a question, or a compound sentence. Watch for repeated _constructions_, not just repeated words — three sentences that all end in an _-ing_ clause is a pattern worth breaking.
- **Talk to the reader.** Plain speech over officialese: "It is imperative that we arrive at the designated location" → "We need to be there on time." Reach for a big word only when its precision or characterization is worth the friction it costs the reader.

Done when every marked spot is either revised or deliberately kept, and every edit traces to a rule above.

## 3. Output

Deliver in one message:

1. The revised text.
2. A short note of what changed and why — grouped by rule, not line by line.

Done when the reply carries both the revised text and the note.
